Storms test every seal on a house. Shingles lift in strong wind, flashing fails around chimneys and vents, and rain driven sideways pushes through window frames and siding.

The water that gets in rarely shows up where it entered. It runs along rafters, soaks insulation and appears as a stain two rooms away. Storm water cleanup starts with finding the path and getting the structure dry.

After a storm, before you do anything else

  • Stay out of rooms with a sagging ceiling or water near light fixtures.
  • Place buckets under drips and move belongings away from wet walls.
  • Photograph roof damage from the ground, interior water and any broken windows, then call.

Emergency Storm Water Damage: First Hours

Protect people first. Keep clear of sagging ceilings and wet electrical fixtures. If the roof is open, a temporary cover from a roofer limits more rain from coming in while you arrange repairs.

Then reduce the water already inside. Move furniture, lift rugs and collect drips. Our crews extract the water, remove wet insulation and set drying equipment while the roofer works.

ANSI/IICRC S500 notes that outside water that touched soil or debris is handled differently from clean rain through a roof opening, which affects what is cleaned and what is removed.

How Storm Water Moves Through a Home

From the roof

Water enters at damaged shingles, flashing and vent boots. It runs down rafters and soaks insulation before it stains a ceiling. By the time the stain shows, the attic has been wet for a while.

Through walls and windows

Driven rain pushes past worn caulk and failed window trim. The water runs down the inside of the wall and collects at the bottom plate.

From the ground

Heavy rain can send surface water toward foundation walls and window wells. If drainage is poor, water enters low rooms. See our basement page for below grade cleanup.

Storm Water Versus Floodwater

Rain that enters through a roof is generally cleaner than ground water. Water that comes in from outside across soil is treated as contaminated. The difference changes what can be saved.

Insurance policies also treat them differently. Wind and rain damage through a damaged roof is often covered, while surface flood water often is not. Read your policy and ask your agent.

Local Storm Patterns and Practical Prep

Southwest Ohio sees thunderstorms, heavy rain and the occasional strong wind event. Clearing gutters before the season, keeping downspouts pointed away from the house and trimming limbs near the roof reduce risk.

Check your attic after a major storm. Look for dark insulation, daylight where there should be none and damp rafters. Early discovery limits the cleanup.

After storm checklist

  • Photograph the roof and yard damage.
  • Cover openings with a tarp if you can do it safely.
  • Call a roofer for repairs.
  • Call us for water removal and drying.
  • Keep receipts for emergency repairs.
  • Contact your insurer with your photos.

Attic and Insulation After a Storm

The attic is the first place storm water lands, and the last place most owners look. Blown in insulation clumps when wet, loses its value and stays damp for weeks. Wet roof sheathing darkens and can soften.

If you can reach the attic safely, look for stains on rafters, damp insulation and rusted nail tips. Photograph them. A roofer and an adjuster will both want to see them.

Crews remove saturated insulation and use dehumidification to dry the framing. New insulation goes in after the wood reads dry.

Documenting Storm Damage for a Claim

Dated photos from the ground, the attic and each wet room tell the story. Write down when the storm hit and when you found the water. Save receipts for tarps, repairs and meals if you had to leave.

Your insurer may send an adjuster. Walk them through the water path. Our moisture readings and equipment logs show what was wet and why materials were removed.

Keep wet materials until the adjuster has seen them unless they are a health risk.
