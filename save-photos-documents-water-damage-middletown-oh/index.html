Save Photos and Documents After Water Damage, Middletown OH

Wet paper and photos can often be saved if you act within a couple of days. Here are the steps.

Published October 29, 2026 · Updated November 12, 2026

Family photos, tax records and legal papers rarely appear on an insurance list, yet they matter more than furniture. They are also fragile in water.

The good news is that paper and photos can often be saved if you act quickly. The key is to stop damage from spreading, dry things gently and avoid mold. This guide covers the basics.

The First 48 Hours

Mold can begin on wet paper within about two days. Move fast. Lift items out of water, take them to a dry place and begin drying or freezing them.

If you cannot start right away, place paper in a freezer. Freezing stops mold and gives you time to plan. This works well for books, files and photos that are stuck together.

For irreplaceable papers, the National Archives and similar preservation guides advise freezing wet items within about two days, and that advice fits the timelines used in ANSI/IICRC S500 for fast response.

Photographs

Photos are more fragile than paper because the emulsion can stick to other surfaces. Gently rinse off mud with clean cool water. Do not rub the surface.

Lay photos face up on clean paper towels or a screen. Keep them out of direct sunlight. Do not stack them while wet. If photos are stuck together, freeze them and separate later or ask a professional conservator.

Steps for wet photos

  • Rinse mud with cool, clean water.
  • Lay face up on absorbent paper.
  • Do not stack or hang by one corner.
  • Keep out of direct sun.
  • Freeze if you cannot dry within a day or two.

Documents and Books

Documents can be air dried on clean towels or racks. Interleave paper towels in books and change them as they get wet. Standing a book on its end with pages fanned helps air flow.

Freeze very wet books and files, then dry them later. Do not use a microwave or oven, which can warp pages and set ink.

When Mold Has Started

Mold on paper is a health concern. Wear gloves and a mask and handle items outdoors if possible. Do not wipe moldy paper in a room you use. Freeze items and consult a conservator for valuable pieces.

For ordinary documents, copy what you need and discard the original if it is too far gone. Keep copies in a safe place.

Protect Records Before the Next Event

Scan important documents and store copies in the cloud. Keep originals in a waterproof, fire rated box on an upper floor. Store photos in archival boxes off the floor.

Make a list of key documents and where they are. If you have to leave quickly, you will know what to grab.

  • Scan IDs, deeds, insurance policies and tax returns.
  • Back up family photos.
  • Use plastic bins with lids, not cardboard.
  • Store boxes on shelves, not on the basement floor.
  • Keep a short inventory of important papers.

When to Call a Professional

Valuable items such as heirloom photos, legal documents and rare books deserve a conservator. Restoration companies can also help with document drying in larger losses. Ask about options when you call.

Keep a record of what was lost for your insurer. Photographs of damaged items help.

Which Documents Matter Most

  • Government IDs and passports.
  • Birth, marriage and death certificates.
  • Property deeds, titles and mortgage papers.
  • Insurance policies and claim records.
  • Tax returns and financial statements.
  • Medical records and prescriptions.
  • Wills and powers of attorney.

For each document, know how to get a replacement. Many agencies can reissue records, but it takes time. Having a scanned copy speeds up the process.

If an original is wet, do not throw it away. Many offices want to see damaged originals when you request replacements.

Digitizing Your Records

A phone camera can scan documents in a few minutes. Use a free scanning app, save each file with a clear name and store copies in two places, such as the cloud and an external drive. Add a password to files with sensitive information.

Photos can be scanned at home or by a local service. Start with the oldest and most irreplaceable prints and negatives.

Update your digital archive once a year. Add new documents and remove old ones that no longer matter.

Working With a Conservator

For heirloom photographs, rare books or legal documents, a professional conservator can stabilize and restore items. Ask about their experience with water damage and about costs before you start.

Your local library, historical society or museum may be able to refer you to a conservator. Freeze wet items while you arrange help.

Recap: Act Fast, Dry Gently

Within two days, either dry papers and photos on clean absorbent surfaces or put them in the freezer. Do not stack, scrub or use heat. For valuable pieces, ask a conservator.

Scan what you can and store a second copy somewhere else. A fifteen minute scan today can save weeks of replacing records later.

Middletown Note: Basements and Family Archives

Many Middletown homes have basements where families store old photos, papers and keepsakes. Those boxes sit near the floor, where seepage and sump pump failures hit first. Moving them to a shelf or upper floor takes an afternoon and protects things that cannot be replaced.

If you keep records for a family business or a rental property, consider digital copies stored away from your home. A river valley storm can affect more than one building at a time, and an offsite copy is the safest protection.
